Who Gets HUD Homelessness Funding? The Court Fight and Arizona’s Next Steps

Source check: November 24, 2026 · FY2026 competition only

Imagine two local nonprofits applying for federal homelessness grants. One wants to keep paying rent and providing support for people who already live in its permanent housing program. The other wants to start a new transitional housing program. Both meet the basic requirements. The first scores higher, but the second may get funded first because the federal rules reserve money for projects in its category.

That was the dispute in two lawsuits over the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development’s 2026 homelessness funding competition. On November 20, a federal judge ruled that HUD went beyond what Congress allowed when it reserved $1.3 billion for certain new projects. The decision addressed who gets a chance to compete. It did not award a grant, open an apartment or move anyone up a housing waitlist. November 20 memorandum and order, pp. 9–18

Three dates, three different actions

  • November 20 | The court ruled on the grant rules. Judge Mary S. McElroy struck the $1.3 billion preference and several other provisions. She did not cancel the entire 2026 competition or simply restore the previous year’s rules. The written decision directed HUD to give regional coalitions another chance to submit Tier 2 applications. As written that day, it called for Tier 1 awards by December 1, Tier 2 submissions through December 1, Tier 2 awards by December 31, and an initial HUD compliance report by November 25. Court memorandum, pp. 14–18 and 57–59
  • November 23 | The court set aside the separate judgments. The judge had issued a written memorandum and order explaining her decision, and separate judgments formally recording the outcome in each case. The November 23 docket orders set aside those judgments. They did not explain why or expressly withdraw the written memorandum. That is not evidence that the judge reversed her reasoning or restored HUD’s $1.3 billion preference. It also does not tell applicants what schedule to follow now. See the November 23 docket entries in cases 1:26-cv-00436 and 1:26-cv-00439, U.S. District Court for the District of Rhode Island.
  • November 24 | The groups that sued asked for more time. In a joint letter, the plaintiffs in both cases asked for 60 days after HUD announces revised Tier 2 rules. They said communities need time to invite providers to apply, review their proposals and submit new local rankings. They also asked the judge to clarify that early Tier 1 awards should go only to projects the local coalitions themselves put in Tier 1. A request is not a ruling. The dates in the November 20 decision describe what it said that day; applicants should confirm the current schedule in later court orders and HUD instructions. November 24 joint letter · HUD competition page

Who competes and how does the money reach people?

Congress funds the Continuum of Care program. HUD issues a Notice of Funding Opportunity, the rulebook for the grant competition. A local Continuum of Care is a coalition that coordinates a region’s homelessness response. Nonprofits and other eligible providers submit proposals to start or renew specific projects. The coalition reviews and ranks those proposals, and its designated lead submits the local application and ranked list. HUD decides which grants to award. People seeking housing don’t enter this competition. They use a separate local intake or referral process, often called coordinated entry. Federal application rules · Tucson/Pima competition records

  • Tier 1 is the part of the local ranked list HUD considers first, subject to eligibility and other applicable checks. For 2026, HUD set its Tier 1 line at 60% of the eligible dollars needed to renew each coalition’s existing grants, the minimum Congress required. If those grants totaled $10 million, the line would be $6 million. That is a share of dollars, not a share of residents, apartments or projects.
  • Tier 2 is the more competitive national round. It can include both new and renewing projects. The local ranking matters, but HUD also uses scores and federal selection rules. Being on a local list is not an award. 2026 funding notice and selection rules

What was the $1.3 billion set-aside?

A set-aside is money reserved for a specific purpose. In a different sense, when a court sets aside a rule or a judgment, it cancels that legal action. The same phrase describes two different things here.

HUD reserved roughly a third of the national funding, from money available after Tier 1, to give new projects priority in Tier 2. After certain earlier priorities, HUD would select eligible new transitional housing and supportive-services-only projects first, then other eligible new projects if needed to reach $1.3 billion. Transitional housing offers a temporary place to stay. Services-only projects can help with outreach, referrals or treatment connections, but do not themselves provide housing. Permanent supportive housing combines housing with no preset time limit and services for people with disabilities who have experienced homelessness. Rapid rehousing helps people move into permanent housing, usually with time-limited rent assistance and services. The reserve was a preference over who could compete for which dollars first, not an across-the-board cut to local grants. Funding notice, § V.D.2 · HUD program regulations

What did each side argue?

HUD said Congress allowed it to add criteria that could make the program more effective. It argued that favored projects still had to qualify, that other projects could compete for the remaining money, and that the new rules emphasized service engagement, recovery and self-sufficiency. HUD also warned that reopening applications put federal award timing at risk.

The plaintiffs, including the City of Tucson in one case and the State of Arizona in the Washington-led case, argued that HUD could add criteria but could not use them to sidestep the factors Congress required, such as past performance. They said the preference changed which projects applied in the first place: a local board might have turned away a permanent-housing proposal, or a provider might not have applied if it saw little chance of winning. Removing the preference after applications closed would not bring those projects back into the running.

The judge agreed that the reserve exceeded HUD’s authority. After the earlier selection steps left in place, otherwise eligible new and renewal projects could compete by score for remaining Tier 2 money. The court did not ban transitional housing or say permanent housing must win. It also rejected certain points for making services mandatory because HUD hadn’t adequately considered the disadvantage to domestic-violence programs that cannot require participation under other federal grants. It left some other service-related criteria intact. The court struck additional provisions involving harm reduction, law enforcement, self-sufficiency, grant screening, bonuses and executive-order requirements, but did not grant every challenge. Federal selection law · Court memorandum, pp. 9–59

What should Arizona readers watch?

For a provider, a late or lost renewal can affect whether it can keep paying rent assistance, retain staff or accept referrals. For a person already housed, the concern is whether that support continues. For someone looking for housing, the concern is whether programs have openings. Those are real risks, but the court documents do not establish that a particular Arizona household lost housing or that any provider won or lost a grant.

Maricopa’s regional board announced a limited local reopening for currently funded permanent-housing projects seeking renewal. That lets certain providers be reconsidered locally; it is not a federal award or an intake opening. In Tucson/Pima and Maricopa, the records that will show what changed are revised local rankings, actual HUD award lists, grant end dates and any documented changes in services or referrals. For people trying to find housing now, use local Housing Help resources rather than the federal grant portal. Maricopa competition notice · Tucson/Pima competition records
